How Wordle Repeated-Letter Feedback Really Works

Why can one copy of a letter turn green while another turns gray? The answer is a counting rule, not a contradiction. Here's a precise example you can reproduce with our Wordle Helper.

A three-E guess against a two-E answer

Imagine the secret word is LEVEL. You guess EERIE. The secret contains two E's; your guess contains three. Only two of the guessed E's can get positive feedback.

  1. Check exact positions first. The second letter of EERIE is E, and the second letter of LEVEL is E, so position 2 is green. One E has now been used.
  2. Look for other present letters. The first E in EERIE is not at its correct position, but another E remains in LEVEL at position 4. The first tile is yellow.
  3. Mark unmatched copies gray. The final E in EERIE is extra. Both E's in LEVEL have already been accounted for, so this tile is gray.
  4. Check the other letters. R and I do not appear in LEVEL. Both are gray.
  • Guess: E E R I E
  • Feedback: 🟨 🟩 ⬛ ⬛ ⬛
  • Answer used for this demonstration: L E V E L

That feedback does not mean all E's have been excluded. It means exactly two E's were matched, and the third guessed E was extra. This distinction matters when narrowing candidates.

Try the exact example in the helper

Open the interactive Wordle Helper and find the section called “Use your previous guesses.” Enter EERIE in the previous-guess field. Each tile begins gray. Tap the first tile once to make it yellow, then the second tile twice to make it green. Leave R, I, and the final E gray. Select “Add guess.”

The tool evaluates every candidate with the same two-pass letter-counting process. A candidate remains only if it would produce exactly the colors you entered. You can record multiple guesses, remove individual ones, or combine them with manual clue filters.

For a second example, try hypothetical answer CIVIC and guess ICICI. The feedback is yellow, yellow, yellow, yellow, gray. CIVIC has only two I's, so the third guessed I cannot also be yellow.

The two-pass rule behind the colors

First, compare a guess with a possible answer one position at a time, marking all exact matches green and subtracting those letters from the remaining letter counts. Second, go left to right over the unmarked tiles. Mark a tile yellow only if its letter is still available in the remaining counts; otherwise mark it gray.

This order is essential. If a solver awards yellow tiles before it handles greens, it can assign one answer letter to two guessed tiles and display candidates that the real feedback rules would reject.

Our helper uses this exact feedback comparison for the whole-guess feature. Its older manual green, yellow, and gray fields are still available for quick searches, but they are less precise for complicated repeated-letter clues because they do not encode every tile of every prior guess.

Choosing your next guess

The tool also shows a small list of candidate words with useful letter coverage. For each remaining dictionary word, it counts each distinct letter once, then favors candidates whose letters appear in more of the remaining possibilities. This favors information gathering, but it is not a mathematical proof that a suggested guess is optimal.

Sometimes the fastest next move is a word that tests several new letters. Other times, when only one or two plausible answers remain, trying an answer directly is sensible. Use the remaining list and your own game strategy together.
